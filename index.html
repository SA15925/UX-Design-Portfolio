Case study 01 · Kanvas × Vogue Future Creators Lab Winner · Best UX Design

Vogue Studio: room for your next draft

A website where high school creators share work in progress and get feedback specific enough to act on.

My role
UX designer and researcher
Client
Kanvas × Vogue Future Creators Lab
Timeline
Design sprint, Oct 2026
Methods
Persona, journey map, HMW, prototyping

Presented to judges from Apple Tina Tang and Kodi Agbasi

The problem

Teen creators hold back unfinished work because they fear being judged. When they do share it, they get “looks good,” which doesn't help them improve.

Kanvas × Vogue works with young creators from underrepresented communities, where mentorship and expert feedback are hard to come by. The alternative is expensive: one art school portfolio coaching package I found costs $1,999, with only 15 spots a season.

Who I designed for

Jules, 16, grade 11. She makes traditional art and wants guidance on one detail of her piece. She is afraid of being judged because it isn't finished, and the feedback she gets now is “looks good.”

The insight that shaped everything: students think reviewers want perfection, but reviewers want to see experimentation and growth. So I designed a space where unfinished work is the point.

Journey map

How might we

How might we help young creators feel safe sharing unfinished work and get specific feedback they can use?

I set aside two alternatives. “Make students more confident artists” was too broad, and “add a comment section to art posts” jumped straight to a solution. Mine names both halves of the problem: feeling safe, and feedback that helps.

Design decisions

I borrowed one strength from each of three platforms: LinkedIn's record of growth, Instagram's visual feed, and Reddit's threaded communities. Every major choice was a deliberate trade-off.

The prototype

I prototyped the core task: a student shares an unfinished design, gets feedback, and posts the next version.

  • Studio feed. Tabs for For you, Connections and Needs feedback. Cards show the student's question and critique counts, never like counts.
  • Share a draft. Upload, file into a project folder, choose a stage (sketch, draft, almost done), and pick who can respond: classmates, teachers, mentors, professionals or KxV staff.
  • Post and annotated feedback. Numbered pins on the image match numbered comments, each written with a sentence starter.
  • Profile as portfolio. Project folders split into In Progress and Completed, with first and final versions side by side.

Accessibility was a checklist item on every screen: 4.5:1 text contrast, 16px minimum body text, 44 × 44 px touch targets, and no information carried by color alone.

Measuring success

  • Students post unfinished work, not just final pieces.
  • Most feedback uses a sentence starter or an annotation.
  • Students come back to post a second version.
  • Students finish with a portfolio that shows growth.

A pilot would need one lab cohort with guardian permission, mentors who commit to response times, and private-by-default profiles with reviewed comments.

What I learned

Students fear sharing more than they lack skill, and small choices like hiding like counts lower that pressure more than any feature. Next time I'd test with real lab students before polishing screens, and treat safety and accessibility as constraints from day one instead of a final pass.

Case study 02 · Usability and accessibility evaluation

“Wait, do I get on now?” Evaluating Google Maps on the highway

An expert review of Google Maps driving navigation, grounded in my own drives through Houston's freeways and frontage roads.

Solo evaluator
Course
Human-Computer Interaction
Timeline
Sept 2026
Methods
Heuristic review, WCAG audit, comparative analysis

Watch

Watch the video essayLive demo · closed captions · audio descriptions · opens in a new tab

Why this app

Houston highways stack several lanes next to parallel frontage roads, and that's where I kept getting confused: stay on the freeway, or take the ramp? I wanted to know whether that confusion was me or the interface, so I evaluated it against two of Shneiderman's Eight Golden Rules and two WCAG 2.1 success criteria, using my own screenshots and screen recordings as evidence.

Findings

1

Lane guidance contradicted the real road

Golden Rule 3 · Offer informative feedback

At one intersection the overhead sign showed two lanes I could use to turn right. Maps showed only one. When feedback is wrong, it's worse than none, because drivers trust it.

Fix: accurate, consistent lane confirmation at every decision point.

2

Directions turned into bare distances

Golden Rule 8 · Reduce short-term memory load

After the first two turns, Google's steps dropped street names entirely: turn left, 50 feet; turn right, 150 feet. On the same kind of route, Apple Maps described what to look for, such as a sidewalk or a path. That's recognition instead of recall.

Fix: landmark-based directions that name something the driver can see.

3

Highway and frontage road were nearly the same color

WCAG 2.1 · 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast (AA)

I sampled the two road surfaces at a highway split and checked them in WebAIM's contrast checker. While I was evaluating it, the driver next to me looked at the screen and asked out loud whether we were supposed to merge yet.

Fix: distinct, high-contrast colors for ramps, lanes and frontage roads.

4

Gemini sits right next to Exit

WCAG 2.1 · 2.5.2 Pointer Cancellation (A)

The Gemini button and the red Exit button share the bottom bar where a driver's thumb rests. A tap fires immediately, with no way to cancel, and a mis-tap on Exit ends navigation mid-drive.

Fix: more space between the two, and a confirmation before ending navigation.

Finding 3, measured

Deliverable

I presented the findings as a 5 to 7 minute video essay with a live demo. The video itself had to meet the standards it was judging, so it includes corrected closed captions and spoken audio descriptions of every visual.

What I learned

WCAG is written for web content, so applying it to a native map app took judgment, and I said so in the review. I also learned to measure rather than eyeball: automated checkers like WAVE can't scan a phone screen, so I sampled the colors by hand.

Discord for Classrooms

Computer science classes at UH run on Discord, but it was built for gaming communities. We set out to learn where it breaks down for students and the TAs who run class servers.

My role
UX researcher, team project
Users
CS students and TAs
Timeline
Fall 2026
Methods

Progress

  5. NextWireframes to Figma prototype

Research approach

We mapped stakeholders first: students, group admins and TAs as primary users; developers and parents as secondary; advertisers and sponsors as tertiary. Then we wrote an interview guide that starts broad (“tell me about the last time you used Discord for a class”) and narrows into notifications, finding old information, and moderation.

We interviewed computer science seniors and a TA who owns and moderates a course server used for exam review sessions with 50 to 70 attendees.

What we're hearing

Muting a busy server means going into every channel one at a time.

Student · notifications

Busy channels rate about a 6 out of 10 to keep up with. A catch-up view would make it an 8 or 9.

Student · information overload

When Canvas and Discord disagree on an assignment, Discord wins, because the Canvas version may be years old.

Student · source of truth

Keeping servers free of spam bots and leaked exams takes a couple of hours a day.

TA · moderation load

Paraphrased from interviews. Participants are anonymized.

Emerging themes

  • Notification overload. Students turn notifications off entirely or switch to Do Not Disturb to study, then miss announcements.
  • Finding old information is manual. People scroll to catch up, and the desktop app lacks the media view that helps on mobile.
  • Pseudonyms lower the barrier to asking. Students ask questions on Discord they wouldn't ask under their real name in Teams.
  • TA presence drives engagement. Servers stay active when TAs answer; they go quiet when instructions are vague.
  • Big servers overwhelm newcomers. Verification steps and dozens of channels keep new members from ever posting.

Next

We're synthesizing these into an affinity map, persona and user story, then running an expert review and taking a redesign from paper wireframes to an interactive Figma prototype. I'll update this case study as it develops.
